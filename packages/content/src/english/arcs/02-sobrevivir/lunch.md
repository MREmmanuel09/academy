**Duration:** ~12 minutes

## Scene 2: Talking about the week (3 min)

> **David:** "So, how was your first week at work?"

> **Maria:** "Really good! Everyone is so welcoming. I went to three meetings, and I didn't understand everything, but I think I'm starting to pick up the rhythm."

> **David:** "That's normal. It takes about a month to feel comfortable in a new job. What did you find most difficult?"

> **Maria:** "Honestly, the small talk. I can follow the work, but when people talk about their weekends or their kids, I get lost."

> **David:** "Ah, I understand. British small talk is its own special skill. It can be very confusing because it sounds like a real conversation, but it's actually just a ritual. People don't really care about the answer. They just want to be friendly."

> **Maria:** "So when someone asks 'How are you?', they're not really asking?"

> **David:** "Exactly. 'How are you?' is more like a greeting. The expected answer is 'Fine, thanks. And you?' It's like a little dance."

### Grammar note: present perfect for experiences

> "It **takes** about a month to feel comfortable."
> "What did you find most difficult?"

We use present perfect to talk about experiences up to now:
- "I have lived in London for 3 weeks." (experience so far)
- "Have you ever eaten British food?" (any time in your life)

We use past simple for completed actions at a specific time:
- "I went to three meetings." (this week)
- "What did you find most difficult?" (in the past)

Rule of thumb: present perfect = "so far in your life," past simple = "at a specific time."

## Scene 3: Small talk in action (4 min)

> **David:** "Let me show you. The barista just asked how I am. Watch what I say."

> **Barista:** "Hi! How are you today?"

> **David:** "Not bad, thanks. How are you?"

> **Barista:** "Good, thanks! What can I get you?"

> **David:** "I'll have a flat white, and one of those brownies. How much is that?"

> **Barista:** "That's £5.50 please."

> **David:** "Here you go. Thanks!"

> **David (to Maria):** "See? No real information exchanged. But we're both happy."

> **Maria:** "That feels strange. In Spain, if you ask someone how they are, you really want to know!"

> **David:** "Ha! I know. It took me a while to understand Spanish culture too. In Spain, small talk is more personal. In the UK, it's more like a polite script."

### Grammar note: politeness markers in requests

> "**Can** I have a flat white, please?"
> "**Could** I have one of those brownies?"
> "**Would** you like a coffee?"

Three politeness levels in requests:

| Level | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | "I want X." | "I want a coffee." |
| Friendly | "Can I have X?" | "Can I have a coffee, please?" |
| Polite | "Could I have X?" | "Could I have a coffee, please?" |
| Very polite | "Would you like X?" | "Would you like a coffee?" |

In a café, "Can I have..." is the most natural. "Could I have..." sounds too formal. "I want..." sounds rude.

## Scene 4: Talking about food (3 min)

> **Maria:** "I have a question. The menu says 'bangers and mash.' What is that?"

> **David:** "Bangers are sausages. Mash is mashed potatoes. It's a very British dish. We also call sausages 'bangers' because they sometimes make a 'bang' when you cook them."

> **Maria:** "Why do they make a bang?"

> **David:** "Because they have a lot of water inside, and when you cook them, the water makes steam, and sometimes they explode!"

> **Maria:** "Oh! That sounds dangerous."

> **David:** "It can be. You have to be careful. But they taste really good. Want to try some next time?"

> **Maria:** "Yes! I'd love to. What else is very British?"

> **David:** "Well, there's fish and chips, of course. And Sunday roast with Yorkshire pudding. And shepherd's pie. And a full English breakfast."

> **Maria:** "What's Yorkshire pudding?"

> **David:** "It's not really a pudding. It's more like a savory pancake. But it tastes amazing with gravy."

### Cultural note: British food

> British cuisine has a bad reputation abroad, but it's actually quite good. Famous dishes include:
> - **Fish and chips** — battered fried fish with chunky chips (thick fries)
> - **Sunday roast** — roasted meat (beef, chicken, lamb) with potatoes, vegetables, and gravy
> - **Shepherd's pie** — minced lamb with vegetables, topped with mashed potato
> - **Full English breakfast** — eggs, bacon, sausages, beans, mushrooms, tomatoes, toast
> - **Bangers and mash** — sausages with mashed potatoes
> - **Yorkshire pudding** — savory batter baked in the oven

> **Pudding** in British English often means "dessert" in general, not just the soft sweet dessert. "What's for pudding?" means "What's for dessert?"

> **Chips** in British English = thick-cut fries. **Crisps** = potato chips (thin and crunchy).
