# Episode 4.2: The sorry habit

**Duration:** ~13 minutes
**Target level:** A2
**Setting:** Maria's first week. Multiple small scenes showing different uses of "sorry".

---

## Characters in this episode

- **Maria** — Confused by how often British people say "sorry".
- **Tom, Priya** — Colleagues who try to explain.
- **Margaret** — The bus stop woman, again.
- **Stranger on the street** — Multiple.

---

## The structure

> **Narrator:** "This episode has 5 short scenes. Each one shows a different way British people use 'sorry'. Watch how each 'sorry' means something different."

---

## Scene 1: At the bus stop (3 min)

> **Maria:** "Excuse me, do you know if the 24 goes to Camden?"

> **Margaret:** "Sorry, I don't. I'm not from round here."

> **Maria:** "Oh, sorry to bother you."

> **Margaret:** "No, no, don't be sorry. Try the man over there with the red coat. He looks like a local."

> **Maria:** "Thanks. Sorry — I mean, cheers."

> **Margaret:** "Ha. You'll get the hang of it."

### What just happened?

> **Narrator:** "How many 'sorry's did you count? Five. Let me break them down:
> 1. 'Sorry, I don't' = 'I don't know' (apology for not having info)
> 2. 'sorry to bother you' = 'I'm sorry for interrupting' (actual apology)
> 3. 'don't be sorry' = 'it's fine, no need to apologize' (reassurance)
> 4. 'Sorry — I mean, cheers' = apology for the slip
> 5. There's an implied sixth in the 'ha' — Margaret is amused
>
> In Spain, you might say 'sorry' once. In the UK, you'll say it 5-10 times a day. It's almost punctuation."

---

## Scene 2: Bumping into someone (2 min)

**[Audio: footsteps, sudden collision]**

> **Stranger:** "Sorry!"

> **Maria:** "Sorry!"

> **Both:** "[laughing]"

> **Stranger:** "My fault, I wasn't looking."

> **Maria:** "No, no, mine. I was on my phone."

### What just happened?

> **Narrator:** "When you bump into someone in the UK, both people say sorry. Even if it's clearly the other person's fault. This is automatic politeness. It's not an admission of guilt — it's a social formula. Refusing to say sorry is considered rude."

---

## Scene 3: Not understanding (2 min)

**[Audio: train announcement, muffled]**

> **Maria:** "Sorry, what was the next stop?"

> **Stranger:** "Oxford Circus."

> **Maria:** "Sorry?"

> **Stranger:** "OXFORD. CIRCUS."

> **Maria:** "Right, thanks. Sorry — I didn't catch it."

> **Stranger:** "No worries, love."

### What just happened?

> **Narrator:** "When you don't understand, you say 'Sorry?' with rising intonation. It's not an apology — it's a request to repeat. The stranger raised their voice on the second try. This is normal. Don't feel bad asking twice."

> **Cultural note:** "If you don't catch it a third time, the British way is to smile and look it up later. Asking a third time is sometimes too much."

---

## Scene 4: Asking for help in a shop (3 min)

**[Audio: shop door, customer]**

> **Maria:** "Sorry to bother you, do you have this in a small?"

> **Shop assistant:** "Sorry, we're out of small. Medium?"

> **Maria:** "Yes, please. Sorry, do you take card?"

> **Shop assistant:** "Sorry, cash only."

> **Maria:** "Oh. Right. Sorry, where's the nearest ATM?"

> **Shop assistant:** "Just round the corner on the left."

> **Maria:** "Brilliant. Cheers."

### What just happened?

> **Narrator:** "Eight 'sorry's in 30 seconds. None of them are real apologies. They're all social glue:
> - 'Sorry to bother you' = polite opener
> - 'Sorry, we're out' = 'unfortunately'
> - 'Sorry, do you take card?' = polite request
> - 'Sorry, cash only' = 'unfortunately not'
> - 'Sorry, where's the ATM?' = polite request again
>
> This is normal. Don't try to stop saying it. The British will think you're cold."

---

## Scene 5: At the office — the big apology (3 min)

**[Audio: meeting room]**

> **Tom:** "Right, I just want to say sorry for the confusion on the Smith project last week. I dropped the ball on that one. Should have caught it earlier."

> **Priya:** "No need to apologize. We all missed it."

> **Tom:** "Still, I want to be clear: I should have flagged it on Tuesday. Won't happen again. Anyway, on to this week..."

### What just happened?

> **Narrator:** "This is a real apology. Notice the difference:
> 1. Specific: 'I dropped the ball on the Smith project'
> 2. Specific: 'Should have caught it on Tuesday'
> 3. Forward-looking: 'Won't happen again'
>
> The British style of apologizing at work is specific, brief, and forward-looking. It doesn't include excessive self-criticism. 'I'm so sorry, I'm the worst' is American-style; British style is 'I should have done X. I'll do Y next time.' End of apology. Move on."

> **Maria's voiceover:** "Tom apologized. We moved on. In Spain, I think we'd still be talking about it."

## Gramática: modals of advice

> **Tom:** "I should have flagged it on Tuesday."

Pattern: `should have + past participle` = "debería haber (hecho) algo pero no lo hice"

- "I should have told you." (debí decirte)
- "You shouldn't have worried." (no debiste preocuparte — usado para consolar)
- "I shouldn't have said that." (no debí haber dicho eso)

> **Priya:** "We all missed it."

Pattern: "We all + past tense" = "todos (nosotros)..." — incluye al hablante.

---

## Cultura: el "sorry" británico

> **Narrator:** "Why so many sorrys? Linguists have a few theories:
> 1. Historical — the British class system rewards humility over confidence.
> 2. Social glue — saying sorry is a way to soften any interaction.
> 3. Face-saving — both parties say sorry, so nobody has to be 'the one at fault'.
>
> Some Britons find it excessive too. There's a famous British joke: 'I was going to apologize, but then I realized I was British, so I said sorry about the fact I was going to apologize.'
>
> If you forget to say sorry, don't panic. People will understand you're not a native speaker. Just smile."
