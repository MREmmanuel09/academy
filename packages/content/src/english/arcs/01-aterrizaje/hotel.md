## Scene 2: Checking in (4 min)

> **Maria:** "Hi, I have a reservation. My name is Maria Garcia."

> **Receptionist:** "Welcome, Maria. Let me check... yes, here you are. A double room for six nights, is that right?"

> **Maria:** "Yes, that's correct."

> **Receptionist:** "Could I see your passport and a credit card, please?"

> **Maria:** "Sure, here you go."

> **Receptionist:** "Thank you. Could you fill in this form, please? Just your name, address, and signature."

> **Maria:** "Of course."

> **Maria:** "Thank you. Is there Wi-Fi?"

> **Receptionist:** "Yes, of course. The network is 'Strand-Guest' and the password is 'welcome2026'."

> **Maria:** "Perfect, thank you."

### Grammar note: "Could you...?" for polite requests

> "**Could** I see your passport?"
> "**Could** you fill in this form?"

"Could you...?" is a polite way to ask someone to do something. Other options:

| Politeness | Example |
|---|---|
| Very polite | "Would you mind filling in this form?" |
| Polite | "Could you fill in this form, please?" |
| Neutral | "Can you fill in this form?" |
| Direct | "Fill in this form." |

In real life, "Could you...?" is the most common.

### Grammar note: "There is" / "There are"

> "**There is** a big bed, a desk, and a TV."
> "**There are** two pillows on the bed."

- "There **is**" + singular: "There is a bathroom."
- "There **are**" + plural: "There are two chairs."

In conversation, native speakers often use "there's" with plurals (informal):
- "There's two chairs" (informal, common in speech)
- "There are two chairs" (correct, formal)

For learning, use the correct form. In casual conversation, you'll hear both.

### Grammar note: "I'll" for future plans

> "I**'ll** call you tomorrow."

"I'll" is the contraction of "I will". We use it for:
- Spontaneous decisions: "I'll have the coffee, thanks."
- Promises: "I'll be there at 8."
- Predictions: "It'll rain tomorrow."
- Offers: "I'll help you."

For scheduled events, use present simple:
- "The train leaves at 8." (not "The train will leave")

## Cultural note: "fill in" vs "fill out"

> In British English: "fill **in** a form"
> In American English: "fill **out** a form"

Both are correct, just different varieties. The receptionist in this story uses British English.
